Volleyball rules look complicated from the stands, but the core of the game fits on one page. Two teams of six hit a ball over a net. Each team gets up to three touches to send it back. If the ball lands on your side, or you can't return it legally, the other team scores.

Everything else on this page explains the details: scoring, rotation, serving, and the faults referees call most often.

**Which rulebook?** Indoor volleyball in the US is played under several rulebooks: USA Volleyball for club and adult play, the NFHS for high school, the NCAA for college, and FIVB rules internationally. The basics below are the same in all of them. Where they differ, we say so.

## Volleyball rules at a glance

| Rule | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Players | 6 per team on the court: 3 in the front row, 3 in the back row |
| Touches | Up to 3 per side. A block does not count as one of them |
| Scoring | Rally scoring: every rally wins a point, no matter who served |
| Sets | First to 25 points, win by 2. A deciding set goes to 15 |
| Match | Best of five sets (college, pro), or best of three in many club and recreational leagues |
| Serving | From behind the end line, after the referee's whistle |
| Rotation | Players rotate one position clockwise each time their team wins the serve back |
| Ball in or out | A ball that touches any part of the boundary line is in |

## The court and the net

An indoor court is 18 meters long and 9 meters wide (59 by 29.5 feet), split in half by the net. A line 3 meters from the net on each side, the attack line, separates the front zone from the back zone. You'll find all measurements in our [volleyball court dimensions guide](/learn/volleyball-court-dimensions/).

The net is 2.43 meters (7 feet 11⅝ inches) high for men and 2.24 meters (7 feet 4⅛ inches) for women. Youth nets are lower. See [volleyball net height](/learn/volleyball-net-height/) for every level.

## How scoring works

Volleyball uses **rally scoring**. Every rally ends with a point for one team, regardless of who served. If you win a rally while the other team is serving, you score a point *and* win the right to serve. That is called a side-out.

- A regular set goes to **25 points**, and you must win by **2**. If it's 24–24, play continues until one team leads by two, for example 27–25.
- The deciding set (the fifth in a best-of-five match, the third in a best-of-three) goes to **15 points**, also win by 2.

Want to know how long all of this takes? Read [volleyball scoring and match length](/learn/volleyball-scoring/).

## The three-touch rule

Each team may touch the ball up to three times before it goes back over the net. The classic pattern is **pass, set, hit**: the first player passes the serve, the second sets it up, and the third attacks.

A few details beginners often miss:

- **The same player can't touch the ball twice in a row.** The exception is a block: a player who touches the ball on a block may also make the next contact.
- **A block doesn't count as a team touch.** After a block, your team still has three touches.
- **You can't catch or throw the ball.** Every contact must be a clean hit. A ball that visibly comes to rest in your hands is a "lift" or "carry".
- **Double contact is legal on the first touch.** When receiving a hard-driven ball, the ball may hit two parts of your body in one action. On the second and third touches, especially when setting, it's a fault.
- **Any body part may play the ball,** including your feet.

## Serving rules

Every rally starts with a serve. The server stands behind the end line and has 8 seconds after the referee's whistle to contact the ball. Commit to your toss: under international rules, tossing the ball and letting it drop counts as a fault, and most US leagues treat it the same way or allow only a single re-toss.

- The serve must go over the net between the antennas and land in the opponent's court.
- A serve that **touches the net and still goes over** is legal and stays in play. It used to be a fault, but all major rulebooks changed this.
- You can't step on or over the end line before you hit the ball. Jump servers may take off from behind the line and land inside the court.
- The receiving team may not block or attack a serve directly back while the ball is entirely above the net.

Learn the different serves step by step in [how to serve a volleyball](/learn/how-to-serve-a-volleyball/).

## Rotation and positions

At the moment of the serve, players must stand in their correct rotational order. Front-row players must be in front of their back-row partners, and players in each row must keep their left-to-right order. If they don't, the referee calls an **overlap** (rotation fault) and the other team gets the point.

As soon as the ball is served, everyone can move wherever they like. That's why you see setters run to the net and hitters switch sides right after the serve.

Each time your team wins the serve back, all six players rotate one spot clockwise, and the player who moves into the back-right position serves. Our [volleyball rotation guide](/learn/volleyball-rotation/) shows this with a diagram.

## Front row and back row

Only the three front-row players may block, or attack a ball that is completely above the top of the net from in front of the attack line.

Back-row players can still attack. They just have to take off from behind the attack line. This is called a **back-row attack** or "pipe" when it comes from the middle back. Landing in front of the line after a legal takeoff is fine.

## Net and center line faults

These are the faults referees whistle most often:

- **Touching the net.** Touching the net between the antennas while playing the ball is a fault. Your hair or jersey brushing the net by accident, away from the play, is usually not called.
- **Reaching over the net.** Blockers may reach over the net, but only after the opponent has made their attack or used their three touches. You can't play the ball on the opponent's side before they've attacked.
- **Crossing the center line.** Your foot may touch the opponent's court, as long as part of that foot stays on or above the center line. Your whole foot crossing over is a fault. Under FIVB rules, other body parts may touch the opponent's court as long as you don't interfere with their play.
- **Ball out.** The ball is out if it lands completely outside the lines, touches the antenna, or crosses the net outside the antennas.

## Hitting a ball off the net

Players love this one: if your teammate's pass or set hits the net, you can still play it, as long as your team hasn't used all three touches. The net touch itself doesn't count as a touch.

## The libero

The libero wears a different colored jersey and is a back-row defensive specialist. The main libero rules:

- The libero plays only in the back row and may replace any back-row player without a formal substitution.
- The libero can't attack a ball that is entirely above the net, and can't block.
- If the libero sets the ball with their fingers from in front of the attack line, a teammate can't attack that ball above the net.
- Under FIVB rules the libero can't serve. NCAA, high school and many USA Volleyball competitions allow the libero to serve in one rotation.

## Substitutions and timeouts

Substitution limits vary a lot between rulebooks. International rules allow six substitutions per team per set, while US college and high school rules allow many more. Each team usually gets two timeouts per set in FIVB and NCAA play, but high school and club rules can differ. If you coach, read your league's rulebook once. It's worth the hour.

## Beach volleyball is different

Beach volleyball uses two players per side, a smaller court, sets to 21 and a block that counts as a touch. Read the full list in [beach volleyball rules](/learn/beach-volleyball-rules/).

Indoor volleyball needs very little gear: court shoes with good grip, knee pads, and an indoor ball.
