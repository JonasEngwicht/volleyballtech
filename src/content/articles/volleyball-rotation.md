In volleyball, the six players on a team rotate **one position clockwise every time their team wins the serve back** from the other team. Rotation makes sure everyone plays front row and back row, and everyone serves. The order you start in stays the same for the whole set.

## The six zones

Each half of the court is divided into six zones, numbered counter-clockwise starting with the server:

| Zone | Position | Row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Right back (the server) | Back |
| 2 | Right front | Front |
| 3 | Middle front | Front |
| 4 | Left front | Front |
| 5 | Left back | Back |
| 6 | Middle back | Back |

"Left" and "right" are always from your own team's view, looking at the net.

## When do you rotate?

You rotate **only when your team wins a rally that the other team served**. That's called a side-out. Everyone moves one zone clockwise: the player in zone 2 moves back to zone 1 and serves, the player in zone 1 moves to zone 6, and so on.

If your team is already serving and wins the rally, you don't rotate. The same player keeps serving until your team loses a rally.

## The overlap rule

When the ball is served, every player must be in the correct order relative to their neighbors. If not, the referee calls an **overlap** (a rotation fault), and the other team scores.

- **Front to back:** Each front-row player must be closer to the net than the back-row player directly behind them. Zone 4 in front of zone 5, zone 3 in front of 6, zone 2 in front of 1.
- **Left to right:** In each row, the players must stay in order. In the front row, 4 must be left of 3, and 3 left of 2. In the back row, 5 left of 6, and 6 left of 1.

Only neighbors are compared. Players can stand anywhere else on the court, as long as these relationships hold. The server is exempt once they are in the service zone.

The overlap rule applies only at the moment of contact on the serve. **Right after the serve, everyone can move anywhere.** That's when setters run to their spot and hitters switch to the side they attack from.

## Front row, back row: what changes?

Only the three players in zones 2, 3 and 4 may block or attack a ball that is completely above the net from in front of the attack line. Back-row players can still attack if they take off behind the attack line. The [volleyball rules guide](/learn/volleyball-rules/) covers this in detail.

## Rotation systems: 5-1, 6-2, 4-2 and 6-6

A "system" describes how many hitters and setters a team uses. The first number is the number of hitters, the second the number of setters.

### 5-1: one setter, five hitters

The most common system in competitive volleyball. One setter sets in every rotation.

- When the setter is in the back row, the team has **three front-row attackers**.
- When the setter rotates to the front row, there are only two front-row hitters, but the setter can attack or "dump" the second ball over the net.
- In the standard lineup, the setter is opposite the opposite hitter, the two outside hitters are opposite each other, and the two middle blockers are opposite each other. See the right half of the diagram.

**Pros:** Consistent sets from one player, one decision-maker. **Cons:** The team needs a setter who is good enough to play every rotation.

### 6-2: two setters, always three hitters

Two setters, placed opposite each other. Whichever setter is in the back row sets; the one in the front row hits. You always have three front-row attackers.

**Pros:** Three hitters in every rotation. **Cons:** You need two strong setters, and the hitters adjust to two different sets. It also uses more substitutions.

### 4-2: two setters setting from the front row

Two setters opposite each other, but the one in the **front row** sets. Usually the setter sets from the right front or middle front, so only two players attack.

**Pros:** Simple to learn, short distances for the setter. **Cons:** Only two attackers. It's popular with younger teams and recreational leagues.

### 6-6: everyone sets

The player who rotates into a certain spot, often right front or middle front, becomes the setter for that rotation. Great for beginners and kids because everybody learns to set.

## Where does the libero fit in?

The libero replaces a back-row player, most often a middle blocker. When the middle rotates to the back row, the libero comes in. When that spot rotates to the front again, the middle comes back. These replacements don't count as substitutions. Under FIVB rules the libero can't serve, but many US rulebooks allow it in one rotation.

## Serve receive formations

When the other team serves, your players arrange themselves to pass the serve while staying legal. Most teams receive with three passers (often the two outside hitters and the libero) spread across the court, and hide the setter near the net. Beginner teams often use a "W" formation with five passers. The exact arrangement changes in each of the six rotations, which is why coaches spend so much practice time on it.

## Tips for learning your rotation

- **Know your two neighbors.** You only need to stay in order with the players beside you and in front of or behind you.
- **Check the scoreboard and the setter.** Many teams number rotations by where the setter stands: "rotation 1" means the setter is in zone 1.
- **Talk.** Before every serve, a quick "I'm in front of you" avoids most overlaps.
