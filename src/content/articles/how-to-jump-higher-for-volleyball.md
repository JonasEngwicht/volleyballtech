Every hitter wants a higher vertical. The good news is that jumping is trainable. The honest news is that it takes months of steady work, and there are no shortcuts.

This guide gives you the basics. It is general information, not a personal training plan. If you are under 18, have had injuries or are new to strength training, work with a qualified coach.

## The three things that decide how high you jump

1. **Strength.** How much force your legs and hips can produce.
2. **Speed.** How fast you can produce that force.
3. **Technique.** How well your approach and arm swing turn it into height.

Most players have one clear weak spot. Beginners usually gain the most from technique. Players with a good approach usually need strength.

## 1. Fix your technique first

Technique is the cheapest height you will ever gain.

- **Use your arms.** A full, fast arm swing adds real height. Swing both arms back on your second-to-last step and drive them up as you jump.
- **Make the last two steps fast.** A long, low step followed by a quick close turns forward speed into lift.
- **Jump up, not forward.** Drifting into the net wastes height.

Our [four-step approach drill](/drills/approach-footwork/) covers this step by step, and [how to spike a volleyball](/learn/how-to-spike-a-volleyball/) puts approach, jump and arm swing together.

## 2. Build strength

Stronger legs and hips are the foundation. The classic exercises work:

- Squats or goblet squats
- Lunges and split squats
- Hip hinges such as Romanian deadlifts
- Calf raises
- Core work such as planks

Two sessions a week are enough for most players. Learn the movements with light weight first and add load slowly. Good form matters far more than heavy weight.

## 3. Add jump training carefully

Plyometrics teach your body to use strength quickly. Start simple:

- Squat jumps
- Approach jumps at full effort
- Low box jumps (step down, do not jump down)
- Skipping and pogo hops

Keep the numbers low and the quality high. A few sets of three to five all-out jumps, with rest between them, do more than a hundred tired ones. Jumping when exhausted trains you to jump slowly.

## 4. Learn to land

More jumping means more landing. Land on two feet when you can, with soft knees, and keep your knees in line with your toes. Practice it with our [safe landing drill](/drills/safe-landing/).

## 5. Rest

Your body gets stronger between sessions, not during them. In season, practice and matches already include a lot of jumping, so add extra jump training sparingly. Knee or shin pain that lingers is a signal to back off and get it checked.

## How to measure progress

You cannot improve what you do not measure. Three options:

- **Wall and chalk.** Mark your standing reach, then your jump reach. The difference is your vertical. Free and good enough.
- **Phone video.** Film from the side in slow motion to check your technique.
- **Jump trackers and apps.** Wearable sensors and camera apps estimate jump height and count your jumps in training. They are handy for tracking workload, but treat the numbers as estimates.

Test every three to four weeks, at the same time of day and after the same warm-up.

## What does not work

- **Shoes.** Good shoes help you plant and land. They do not add meaningful height. See our [shoes for jumping](/best/best-volleyball-shoes-for-jumping/) for what they can do.
- **Ankle weights and "jump shoes".** There is little evidence they help, and they can strain your joints.
- **Doing everything at once.** Programs that promise ten inches in a few weeks mostly deliver sore knees.

## A simple weekly outline

| Day | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Strength, then a few sets of jumps |
| 2 | Practice or rest |
| 3 | Technique: approach work and full-effort approach jumps |
| 4 | Practice or rest |
| 5 | Strength |
| Weekend | Matches or rest |

Adjust it to your team schedule, and take an easy week when you feel worn down.
